Where the filter is wrong and cannot tell you

A sensor that measures only direction leaves range weakly determined, so the filter's uncertainty stretches along the line of sight where the measurement carries no information. Each scenario runs the same estimator on a different geometry. The two figures beside each name are the error the filter actually made and the error its own runtime check reported. The gap between them is the finding.

NEES compares the error the filter made against the uncertainty it claimed. The state holds position and velocity in two dimensions, four numbers, so an honest filter averages 4. Above that line it is overconfident. NIS compares each measurement against the filter's prediction of it, needs no knowledge of the truth, and is therefore the only one of the two that exists outside a simulator. A scenario where NEES departs and NIS does not is one where the filter is wrong and nothing on board would say so.

Two contours are drawn per filter because a 1σ ellipse in two dimensions holds only 39 per cent of the probability, not the 68 per cent most people picture. That figure is one-dimensional and does not carry over: reaching 68 per cent in a plane takes 1.52σ, and 2.45σ is the 95 per cent contour. Even an honest filter leaves the target outside the inner ellipse most of the time.

The three panels above draw a single run. The last panel and the figures beside each scenario name come from 400 runs, and they count differently on purpose: the sidebar averages over tracks that survived, since a diverged track produces an arbitrarily large error that would swamp the mean, while the last panel averages every run including the ones that were lost. Where nothing is lost the two agree exactly. Where tracks are lost they do not, and the gap is the size of that problem rather than a discrepancy to reconcile.

The green track is the correction, the same filter with the straight-line approximation of the measurement replaced by a fifth-degree cubature rule, on byte-identical measurements. On any single run the difference between the two is mostly noise, which is why the figures beside each scenario name come from hundreds of runs and carry the smallest effect those runs could resolve. Where a scenario reads not resolved, the run count could not separate the effect from noise, and it should be read as a question rather than a result.

In the intercept scenario the purple track carries no sensor. It is vectored entirely on the sentry's estimate, and its aim point is frozen at commit, so whatever the sentry believed at that moment is what the interceptor flies at for the rest of the engagement. Against a perfectly known target it misses by about 46 m, which is the guidance floor. Flown on the tracker it misses by roughly 220 m. Both filters miss by nearly the same amount, and that is worth understanding rather than glossing: the cubature correction repairs the covariance, not the estimate itself, and miss distance is decided by the estimate. A filter can be made honest about its uncertainty without being made more accurate, and only the first of those is what this project fixes.
